# What is the passé composé?

Passé composé is the past tense you'll end up using more than any other in French. If you'd say something like "I did", "I went", "I ate" in English, that's a passé composé in French.

It's called *composé* (compound) because the verb is built from two parts stuck together:

:::subject subject + helper verb + past participle :::

The helper verb is either *avoir* or *être*, conjugated in the present tense. The past participle is the verb you actually mean.

For example:

- **J'ai mangé** une pomme. ("I ate an apple.")
- **Elle est allée** au marché. ("She went to the market.")

The bolded parts are the helper + past participle.

# Building the past participle

The past participle is the bit that tells you what the action was. For regular verbs, the ending depends on the infinitive group.

| Group | Ending | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| -er verbs | -é | parler → parlé |
| -ir verbs | -i | finir → fini |
| -re verbs | -u | vendre → vendu |

/t Most French verbs are in the -er group, so most of your past participles will end in -é. \

Then there's the irregulars. There's no system for these, you just have to learn them. Some of the most common ones:

| Verb | Past participle |
| --- | --- |
| faire | fait |
| voir | vu |
| prendre | pris |
| mettre | mis |
| avoir | eu |
| être | été |
| boire | bu |
| écrire | écrit |
| lire | lu |
| dire | dit |

# Avoir or être?

This is the part most people get caught on. Most verbs use **avoir** as the helper, but a smaller group uses **être** instead.

## When to use être

You use être with:

1. **Reflexive verbs** (the ones with *se* in front: *se laver*, *se réveiller*, *se coucher*…)
2. **Movement and state-change verbs.** The classic way to remember these is the *DR & MRS VANDERTRAMP* mnemonic:

:::blockquote **D**evenir, **R**evenir, **M**onter, **R**ester, **S**ortir, **V**enir, **A**ller, **N**aître, **D**escendre, **E**ntrer, **R**etourner, **T**omber, **R**entrer, **A**rriver, **M**ourir, **P**artir :::

Anything that isn't on that list and isn't reflexive takes avoir.

# Agreement (this only matters for être verbs)

Here's the part that catches everyone out. When the helper is être, the past participle has to **agree with the subject** in gender and number, just like an adjective.

| Subject | Past participle of *aller* |
| --- | --- |
| il / je (masc.) | allé |
| elle / je (fem.) | allé**e** |
| ils / nous (masc. or mixed) | allé**s** |
| elles / nous (fem.) | allé**es** |

So:

- **Il est allé** ("he went")
- **Elle est allée** ("she went")
- **Ils sont allés** ("they went", masc. or mixed group)
- **Elles sont allées** ("they went", all female)

Avoir verbs don't agree with the subject, so for most of the verbs you'll use, you can ignore agreement entirely.

/w There is *one* edge case where avoir verbs take agreement, but only when there's a direct object placed before the verb. Don't worry about it for now, that's its own lesson. \

# How to actually build a sentence

Five steps:

1. Pick your subject. (*Je*)
2. Decide the helper. Is the verb on the être list, or reflexive? If yes, use être. If no, avoir. (*Manger* isn't on the list, so avoir.)
3. Conjugate the helper in the present for that subject. (*J'ai*)
4. Stick the past participle on the end. (*mangé*)
5. If the helper was être, check whether you need to add an -e, -s, or -es for agreement.

So: **J'ai mangé.**

# Common mistakes to watch for

- *J'ai allé* looks tempting, but aller takes être. ||It's *je suis allé*.||
- Don't forget agreement on être verbs. ||*Elle est allé* should be *elle est allée*.||
- Irregular past participles trip people up. ||The past participle of *faire* is *fait*, not *fais*.||
- *J'ai mange* without the accent. ||The past participle of *manger* is *mangé*, not *mange*.||
